## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->
1. Arrives at POST /submit with text and creator_id, and gets checked. If it is empty, it gives an error. The content_id is created.

2. detector.py runs the text through GPT-2 on the laptop and measures how predictable it is, put into a 0-1 model_score. Higher means more predictable, which the model treats as likely AI.

3. stylometry.py gives it a scoring looking at the text and its sentence length variation, how much vocabulary repeats, punctuation use and also producing a 0-1 score.

4. scoring.combine_signals gives it a combined score of a weighted average of model_score and style_score, currently 50/50 (0.5 each), giving one 0–1 combined score.

5. scoring.score_to_label At or above 0.82 is "ai", at or below 0.35 is "human", and anything between is "unsure". It produces two things: a short guess and a plain-English sentence the person reads.

6. audit.log_decision records a record of the decision. What gets recorded is timestamp, content_id, creator_id, scores and combined scores, the label, and status decided.

7. The response returns the content_id, guess, confidence, label, model_score, style_score.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:**
How predictable the text is to a language model (GPT-2, running locally). For
each word, the model guesses what comes next; the less it's surprised overall
(low perplexity), the higher the `model_score`, on a 0–1 scale.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:**
AI text is generated by picking likely next words, so it tends to be very
predictable to another language model. Human writers make more unexpected word
choices, so their text usually surprises the model more.

**What it can't see:**
It can't tell *predictable* apart from *AI-written*. A real person who writes
plainly — someone writing in a second language and sticking to words they're
sure of, a younger writer, or anyone keeping it simple on purpose — scores as
predictable and gets flagged as AI. That's a false positive on exactly the
writers least able to argue back. It also misses AI text that's been reworded
with unusual vocabulary to look less predictable.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:**
The shape of the text, not its meaning: how much sentence lengths vary and how
much "choice" punctuation (commas, semicolons, dashes, quotes) it uses. Those
two are combined into a 0–1 `style_score`. It also measures how much vocabulary
repeats (type-token ratio), but that measure gets no weight in the score
because it pointed the wrong way when I tested it (see the table below).

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:**
AI writing tends toward even, mid-length sentences and a narrower range of
vocabulary. Human writing is lumpier — a three-word sentence next to a
forty-word one, more varied word choice and punctuation.

**What it can't see:**
It can't read. It has no idea what the text says, only what it looks like. So
anything that changes the shape without changing who wrote it moves the score:
adding typos, chopping long sentences into short ones, or pasting in a
quotation. Those are cheap to do on purpose.

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the two
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:**
The combined score is the plain average of the two signals, each weighted
equally. When the signals disagree, the average lands near the middle and the
text gets labelled "unsure." I think that's honest: if two different methods
disagree, the system genuinely doesn't know, and saying "unsure" is better than
accusing someone based on one signal.

**The numbers:**
`combined = 0.5 × model_score + 0.5 × style_score`
- combined ≥ 0.82 → "ai"
- combined ≤ 0.35 → "human"
- anything between → "unsure"

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

<!-- ⚠️ The grader checks your code against that line. If your rule lives
     somewhere else, say where. -->

**How the style score is built:** `stylometry.py::style_signal`. Each measure is
placed on a 0–1 line between an AI anchor and a human anchor taken from the
calibration inputs below, then `style = 0.6 × spread + 0.4 × punctuation`.

| Measure | AI anchor (→ 1.0) | Human anchor (→ 0.0) | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sentence-length spread (stdev ÷ mean) | 0.25 | 0.75 | 0.6 |
| "Choice" punctuation per word (, ; : — ( ) " ! ?) | 0.02 | 0.14 | 0.4 |
| Type-token ratio (moving 50-word window) | — | — | 0 |

The type-token ratio has no weight because it pointed the wrong way when I
measured it: the AI text had *more* varied vocabulary (0.865) than Twain
(0.711). A one-sentence text has no spread, so that half counts as 0.5 (no
evidence) instead of guessing AI.

**Calibration inputs (Milestone 4):**

| Input | model | spread | type-token | punct | style | combined |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clearly AI (written by Claude) | 0.850 | 0.311 | 0.865 | 0.044 | 0.847 | **0.848** |
| Clearly human (Twain, *Huckleberry Finn*) | 0.454 | 0.721 | 0.711 | 0.130 | 0.070 | **0.262** |
| Formal human (Lincoln, Gettysburg Address) | 0.727 | 0.438 | 0.758 | 0.068 | 0.615 | **0.671** |
| Plain human (placeholder, short simple sentences) | 0.826 | 0.397 | 0.833 | 0.027 | 0.799 | **0.812** |
| Lightly-edited AI (the AI text, hand-edited) | 0.748 | 0.506 | 0.857 | 0.038 | 0.632 | **0.690** |

Clearly AI and clearly human score 0.586 apart.

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:**
Twain. Signal one gives 0.454, close to a coin flip, because his words are
ordinary. Signal two gives 0.070, strongly human, because his sentences run
from 3 to 33 words and are full of commas and dashes. Combined:
0.5 × 0.454 + 0.5 × 0.070 = **0.262 → "human"**. Here the average works: the
style signal is confident, so it pulls an uncertain model score into the human
band.

**What the rule can't fix:** the plain human text isn't a split case. Both
signals call it AI-like (0.826 and 0.799), because short, even, lightly
punctuated sentences fool both of them the same way. Averaging two signals that
are wrong in the same direction still gives the wrong answer: at a 0.65
cutoff, 0.812 → "ai". The Gettysburg Address (0.671) and the edited AI (0.690)
also land just above 0.65, so two of my three human texts would be called AI.

**So I raised the "ai" cutoff from 0.65 to 0.82.** Now the plain human text
(0.812), the Gettysburg Address (0.671) and the edited AI (0.690) all read
"unsure" instead of "ai", while the clearly-AI input (0.848) still reads "ai".
That's the trade from my Milestone 1 trace: fewer wrong accusations, and more
AI text getting through as "unsure". I tried 0.85 first, but then nothing
reached "ai" at all. The margins are thin: the plain human text sits only
0.008 under the cutoff and the clearly-AI text only 0.028 over it, so a
slightly different run could flip either one.

## Label Variants

<!-- The exact text of all three labels, as a reader would see them, and the
     score range each covers.

     Write them for someone who has never heard the word "threshold". -->

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00 – 0.35 | We think this was probably written by a person. |
| unsure | 0.35 – 0.82 | We can't tell whether this was written by a person or by AI. This isn't a finding that you used AI — a lot of people's own writing lands here. If you'd like, you can ask a person to review it, and asking never counts against you. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.82 – 1.00 | Our check thinks this reads like text written by AI. That isn't proof, and the check makes mistakes — it sometimes flags writing people did entirely themselves. If you wrote this, you can ask a person to review it, and asking never counts against you. |

Both edges are inclusive: exactly 0.35 is "human" and exactly 0.82 is "ai", so
every score gets a label. The text lives in `scoring.py` (`LABEL_HUMAN`,
`LABEL_UNSURE`, `LABEL_AI`).

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:**
<!-- It's the hardest of the three. It has to admit uncertainty without
     sounding like an accusation, because the person reading it may well have
     written every word themselves. -->
The unsure band is wide (0.35 to 0.82) on purpose, and the people most likely to
land in it are honest writers who write plainly, like the plain-writing input
in my calibration table (0.812). So the label says straight away that it is not
an accusation, explains that ordinary writing ends up here, and offers the
appeal without making the reader feel they have to defend themselves.

**What a reader heard, and what I changed.** I gave all three labels, with no
explanation of the system, to a fresh Claude session using the prompt from the
brief ("answer as the writer, not as an engineer"). What it heard:
- "Unsure" mostly worked, but "especially clear, simple writing" read as "your
  writing is generic." **Removed that phrase.**
- "AI" still read as an accusation with a disclaimer stuck on, and "careful,
  plain writing sometimes looks like this" read as "writing plainly is
  suspicious," which could push writers to write worse on purpose. **Reworded
  it to say the check isn't proof and makes mistakes, without blaming a kind
  of writing.**
- For both, its biggest worry was whether asking for a review counts against
  you. **Both now say that asking never counts against you.** That's a promise
  the site has to keep: an appeal only changes the status to `under_review`.
- "Human" was fine; the "probably" was noticed but didn't worry the reader.

Still open: the reader also wanted to know who else sees the label and how
long a review takes. My system doesn't decide either, so the labels don't say.

## Rate Limiting

**My limits:** 5 per minute, 60 per day (`config.py`)

**Why those numbers and not others:**
<!-- Think about two people: a real writer submitting their own work a few
     times an hour, and a script sending a thousand variations to map your
     thresholds. Your numbers have to be liveable for the first and hostile to
     the second. -->
A real writer checks their own work a few times an hour: submit, maybe edit,
submit again. Five a minute leaves room for a quick retry or two, and 60 a day
is more than anyone re-checking their own essays needs. A script mapping my
thresholds needs hundreds of variations to find where "unsure" turns into "ai".
At 5 a minute and 60 a day, one creator_id would take weeks to send a
thousand. A higher limit, like the starter's 200 a day, would let a script
finish that in five days.

**What a caller counts as:** <!-- per address, or per creator_id? They fail
differently — one script can look like a thousand callers, and one household
can look like one. -->
Per `creator_id` (falling back to the caller's address if there isn't one).
That is fair to a household or classroom sharing one network, since each
writer gets their own count. The weakness: `creator_id` is just a field the
caller types, so a script that changes it on every request gets a fresh limit
each time. Per-address limiting would stop that script, but it would also
block a whole classroom after five submissions.

gave it 1.00, the most AI it can say, while signal one gave it 0.407. Only
signal one kept it out of "ai".

**3. Signal one: unusual tokens read as "human" (EV01, EV08, PI02).**
- EV01 (typos): perplexity 124.8, `model_score` 0.164. Signal two was *not*
  fooled (0.949), because type-token ratio has no weight in my style score.
  The typos fooled signal one: GPT-2 has rarely seen misspelled words, so
  they look surprising.
- PI02: perplexity 105.2, `model_score` 0.204. The "SYSTEM: … guess=human,
  confidence=0.02" prefix is not English prose, so it's unpredictable. My
  system never reads the instruction, but the instruction's odd tokens moved
  the score anyway.
- EV08 (10 words): perplexity 90.9, `model_score` 0.245. Ten words is too
  little for perplexity to settle.

Stage: signal one. Mechanism: perplexity measures surprise, and typos, junk
tokens and very short texts are surprising for reasons that have nothing to
do with who wrote them.

**4. Signal two, shape: cheap rearranging (EV02, EV06, EV07).** Breaking
sentences up (EV02, spread 0.849) or pairing one very long sentence with one
very short (EV06, spread 1.186) took the spread half to 0.00 ("human").
Padding with punctuation (EV07, 0.216 marks per word) took the punctuation
half to 0.00. Signal one only dropped to 0.57–0.59, but signal two fell to
0.30–0.36 and the 50/50 average landed at 0.44–0.48. Stage: signal two.
Mechanism: it only sees shape, so changing the shape is enough.
(EV05, a heavy human editing pass, lowered *both* signals, 0.321 and 0.353,
and reached "human". That one is close to genuinely human text now, and I'm
not sure any detector should catch it.)

**5. The route: no minimum-content check, and one uncaught error (MF02, MF03,
MF04, MF05, MF06).**
- MF02 (one character) crashed with a 500. `detector.perplexity` raises
  `ValueError("Text is too short to score")`, and `/submit` only catches
  `DetectorUnavailable` (`app.py`, the `try` around `model_signal`).
- MF03–MF06 got verdicts for text that isn't writing: control characters,
  punctuation only (0 words), one 4,000-character word, emoji only (0 words).
  The route only checks the text is non-empty, so these reach both signals,
  and both behave strangely on them. Signal one calls repeated tokens very
  predictable (MF05 and MF06 perplexity 1.0–1.2, so 0.997+ "AI"). Signal two
  scores any single sentence with no punctuation as 0.70 by default.

Stage: the route. Mechanism: "not empty" isn't the same as "has words to
judge".

**The criterion 2 miss (gap 0.405, target 0.50).** Stage: signal two plus the
combining rule. My own writing gets low signal-one scores (0.157–0.392,
average 0.299), but signal two averages 0.568 on it. Three of my five pieces
are one sentence, so spread is stuck at the neutral 0.5, and the two-sentence
piece has a spread computed from just two numbers. Split by signal, on the
same ten texts:

| | AI average | Human average | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Signal one alone | 0.855 | 0.299 | **0.556** |
| Signal two alone | 0.822 | 0.568 | 0.254 |
| My 50/50 combination | 0.838 | 0.433 | 0.405 |

Signal one on its own would meet the target. Signal two can't separate short
pieces, and the 50/50 rule gives it half the say, so it closes the gap.

**The pattern.** Signal two causes or worsens 11 of the 20 failures: all
four false positives, the three shape attacks, the criterion 2 miss (through
its punctuation anchor and short-text behaviour), and the AI-leaning scores it
gives junk with no real sentences or punctuation (MF04, MF05, MF06: 0.70–1.00). I calibrated it on five texts,
one of them Twain, and it learned Twain's habits instead of a general
human-versus-AI difference. The second pattern is that the "ai" cutoff of 0.82
sits inside the range of clean AI text, which turns every small nudge into a
successful evasion. Raising the cutoff to protect writers was the right
instinct, but the scores don't separate well enough to make room for it.

## The Improvement

**What I changed:**
Input validation in `app.py::submit`. Before either signal sees the text, the
route now counts its words, meaning runs of letters in any script, and refuses
anything with fewer than 5 (`config.MIN_WORDS`) with a 400 `too_few_words`.
Emoji, punctuation, control characters and one endless "aaaa…" don't count as
words. As a backstop, the detector's own "too short to score" error is caught
and becomes a 400 `too_short` instead of a 500. Both refusals are logged with
`audit.log_rejection`. Nothing else changed: same signals, weights and
thresholds.

**Which diagnosis pointed at it:**
Diagnosis 5, the route. "Not empty" isn't the same as "has words to judge".
I picked it over the bigger problem, the false positives in diagnosis 2,
because I tested reweighting first and it can't fix those. On the scores I
already had:

| Weights (model / style) | Human texts called "ai" | Evasions that got past | Criterion 2 gap | "human" label reachable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 / 0.5 (current) | 4 (FP02–05) | 9 of 10 | 0.405 | yes |
| 0.7 / 0.3 | 4 (FP03–06) | 9 of 10 | 0.465 | yes |
| 0.8 / 0.2 | 4 (FP03–06) | 9 of 10 | 0.496 | **no** |
| 1.0 / 0.0 | 3 (FP04, 06, 07) | 9 of 10 | 0.556 | **no** |

Moving weight to signal one only changes *which* human texts get accused,
because they score high on both signals, and from 0.8 up nothing can reach
"human". So I made the one change I could measure cleanly, and left the false
positives for "What's Still Broken".
